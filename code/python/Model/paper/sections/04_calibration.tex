durable, a heat pump or an electric vehicle. The relevant economy is the European one of Sections~\ref{sec:intro}

The adoption block adds four parameters. The green durable depreciates at
$\delta_g=0.05$, a five-year life, which fixes the steady-state switching flow at
$\bar D^{\mathrm{sw}}=\delta_g\bar D^G$. We anchor the durable to the electric-vehicle
margin, where the operating-cost gap is measured directly. The delivered green/brown
ratio is the electricity cost of driving a battery-electric vehicle relative to the
fuel cost of a comparable gasoline car, $\varrho=(P^G e_G)/(P^B e_B)$. At 2025
euro-area retail prices (electricity \mbox{0.29 EUR/kWh} and gasoline
\mbox{1.62 EUR/L}, both inclusive of taxes) and real-world energy use of
$0.21$~kWh/km for the electric car and $7.0$~L/100km for the gasoline car, this gives
$\varrho=0.54$. Tax-inclusive prices are the relevant ones here: the baseline carries
no carbon tax, so the household compares retail prices, and the asymmetric taxation of
the two fuels is part of the environment the crisis and the ETS act on.
Appendix~\ref{app:rho} lists every input and its source, and
shows the policy ranking is invariant to $\varrho$ across $[0.48,0.80]$. The switching
cost $\psi_g$ is solved to hit a steady-state green share $\bar D^G=5\%$. That leaves one free
parameter, the logit taste scale $\sigma_\varepsilon$, which governs how responsive
the margin is and which a level target cannot pin. Section~\ref{sec:taste_id}
disciplines it.

\subsection{Identifying the taste scale}\label{sec:taste_id}

The switching cost $\psi_g$ and the taste scale $\sigma_\varepsilon$ are not
separately identified by the single steady-state target $\bar D^G=5\%$. For any
$\sigma_\varepsilon$, $\psi_g$ re-solves to hit the same share, so the steady state is
invariant along the $(\sigma_\varepsilon,\psi_g)$ locus
(Table~\ref{tab:taste_identification}): the green share and the switcher mass do not move as
$\sigma_\varepsilon$ ranges over an order of magnitude and $\psi_g$ with it, from
$0.58$ to $1.74$.

The responsiveness of the margin, however, varies along the locus. The
steady-state adoption elasticity, the response of the green share to a permanent
improvement in the green/brown operating-cost ratio, at fixed $\psi_g$, falls from
$4.4$ at $\sigma_\varepsilon=0.02$ to $0.8$ at $0.20$, and the crisis adoption wave
falls with it, from a peak $\Delta D^G$ of $13.8$ to $2.2$ percentage points
(Figure~\ref{fig:taste_identification}, in Appendix~\ref{app:taste}). The adoption-channel magnitudes are therefore conditional
on $\sigma_\varepsilon$: a level moment cannot discipline them, and a second
moment, the adoption elasticity, is required.


The quasi-experimental vehicle literature provides that moment, on the
upfront-cost margin. \citet{GallagherMuehlegger2011} and \citet{Chandra2010} estimate that a
\$1{,}000 tax incentive raises hybrid adoption by 31--38\%, in a population of
higher-income early adopters; \citet{MuehleggerRapson2022} estimate a demand elasticity of
$-2.1$ for electric vehicles among low- and middle-income households, roughly 8\% per
\$1{,}000 at their mean transaction price of \$26{,}000: the mass-market floor on
responsiveness. In the model one unit is quarterly household consumption, taken as
\$20{,}000, so the baseline $\psi_g=0.770$ is a green premium of about \$15{,}400; a
\$1{,}000 cut in $\psi_g$ raises the switching flow by 39\% at
$\sigma_\varepsilon=0.05$, 30\% at $0.07$, and 12\% at $0.20$
(Table~\ref{tab:taste_identification}).

Which end of the empirical range applies depends on who the marginal adopter is.
At a 5\% green share the model's marginal adopters
consume roughly twice the average brown household (Section~\ref{sec:exante}): they
are the higher-income early adopters of the hybrid-era estimates, not the
mass-market population of \citet{MuehleggerRapson2022}, whose estimate therefore bounds
responsiveness from below. Matching the early-adopter range pins
$\sigma_\varepsilon\in[0.05,0.07]$, with an implied green premium of
\$15{,}000--\$18{,}000 within the heat-pump and EV range, whereas
$\sigma_\varepsilon\ge 0.10$ would imply premia of \$22{,}000 and above. The baseline
$\sigma_\varepsilon=0.05$ is the elastic end of that interval, and the mass-market
estimate is spanned by the sensitivity analysis. The operating-cost margin, the
channel \citet{BeresteanuLi2011} document for gasoline prices and hybrid demand, then
implies an elasticity of the green share to the energy-cost gap of $2.7$ at the
baseline. This responsiveness is a structural property of a rare margin rather than an
artefact of the smoothing method; Appendix~\ref{app:taste} develops the point.

The dollar mapping is the one assumption stated rather than estimated. A higher
consumption unit lowers the model's per-\$1{,}000 response and raises the implied
premium, so the two criteria move together and the identified interval stays
narrow. Halving the unit moves the interval toward higher $\sigma_\varepsilon$,
doubling it toward lower; either way the interval lies inside the sensitivity grid
of Table~\ref{tab:taste_identification}, over which the steady state and the policy
ordering are invariant.

The aggregate results do not depend on any of this. The cumulative output loss
$\sum y$ is flat across the locus (Table~\ref{tab:taste_identification}, last column),
varying by under 4\% while the adoption response varies tenfold, and the policy
ordering follows from the relative price alone. The taste scale governs the size of the adoption channel; the cap-versus-transfer
contrast does not depend on it.

\subsection{The experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

This subsection fixes what the rest of the paper holds constant: the two crisis
experiments, the counterfactual that isolates the adoption channel, the policy
instruments, and the welfare measure by which scenarios are ranked.

\paragraph{The two energy shocks.}\label{sec:shocks}

Two experiments drive the crisis, one per energy closure. The \emph{price} shock
doubles the world energy price on impact and lets it decay with a 16-quarter
half-life under perfectly elastic supply,
%
\begin{equation}
P^{E*}_t=\bar P^{E*}\bigl(1+\nu\,\rho^{\,t}\bigr),
\qquad
\rho=2^{-1/h},
\label{eq:price-shock}
\end{equation}
%
with impact size $\nu=1$, a doubling, and half-life $h=16$ quarters, the experiment
of \citet{auclert2023energy}. The \emph{supply} shock removes 10\% of the
available quantity for six quarters and lets the price clear it,
%
\begin{equation}
E^{*}_t=\bar E^{*}\bigl(1-0.10\cdot\I[t<6]\bigr),
\label{eq:supply-shock}
\end{equation}
%
the fixed-quantity closure of \citet{bayer2026hank}. Figure~\ref{fig:shocks} shows the two side by side. Under the price shock we move
the world price directly and the quantity clears; under the supply shock we move
the quantity, a 10\% cut, and the clearing price adjusts. Because energy
demand is highly inelastic ($\eta_E=0.10$), a 10\% quantity cut requires a price
spike of about $170\%$ to clear, so the supply shock is in fact the larger
\emph{price} disturbance of the two on impact. We report the price shock as the primary experiment. Under the fixed-quantity closure
the clearing price must move to clear the market, and a policy that mutes the household
demand response, such as the cap, forces it to move further still
(Section~\ref{sec:dial}); under the price shock the world price is exogenous, so the
policy comparison is free of this price-clearing feedback. The supply shock is
reported alongside it as the fixed-quantity counterpart.
Section~\ref{sec:crisis} puts the adoption margin to work under both.

\paragraph{Isolating the adoption channel.}\label{sec:counterfactual}

Several results below isolate the adoption channel as the difference between the
full model and a ``no-adoption'' counterfactual,
$\Delta_H\equiv\sum_{t<H}(y^{\mathrm{adopt}}_t-y^{\mathrm{no\text{-}adopt}}_t)$.
The counterfactual is constructed to share the full model's steady state
\emph{exactly}: households still hold the discrete durable-choice margin, and the
steady-state green share, switching flow, and switching cost
($\bar D^{G}=5\%$, $D^{sw}_{ss}$, $\psi_g$) are identical to the adoption
economy. What differs is the \emph{transition} only: the discrete-choice
probabilities are held at their steady-state values throughout the impulse
response, so the durable composition does not respond to the crisis while every
other block responds normally. We call this the \emph{common-steady-state}, or frozen-choice, counterfactual. The alternative, shutting the choice by
making green durables infeasible, so the counterfactual's own steady state has
$D^{G}_{ss}=0$, would compare two economies that differ both in the crisis
response \emph{and} in steady-state composition; the frozen construction isolates
the crisis response alone.\footnote{Impulse responses are reported as $100\times$ the
level deviation from steady state. Variables whose steady state is one (prices, $w$,
$n$, and the shares $D^{G}$, $D^{sw}$) read directly as percent or percentage-point
deviations; $C$ (steady state $\approx0.999$) is within rounding of a percent; $y$ and
$\mathrm{gdp}$ (steady state $\approx0.998$) are level deviations $\times100$, within
about $0.2\%$ of the exact percentage; energy quantities are a share of the energy
steady state ($\approx0.040$).}

\paragraph{The policy instruments.}\label{sec:instruments}

The instruments are those of Section~\ref{sec:gov} and Table~\ref{tab:instruments}.
Four are crisis instruments, zero in the baseline and deployed once the shock has
landed: the price cap $\tau^E$ acts on the brown retail price $\PEB$; the Slutsky
transfer $ins_E$ and the flat transfer $T^{\mathrm{unt}}$ act on income, the first
indexed to pre-crisis brown-energy use and the second lump sum; the green subsidy
$s_g$ pays a fraction of the switching cost. All four are deficit-financed and
repaid through the labour tax \eqref{eq:gbc2}. The fifth, the permanent carbon tax
$\tau_b$, is the ex-ante counterpart: it raises $\PEB$ before any crisis and defines
the ``prepared'' economy of Section~\ref{sec:exante}, with its revenue rebated lump
sum. Section~\ref{sec:shield} compares the crisis instruments and
Section~\ref{sec:exante} the ex-ante one; the distinction that organises both is
whether the instrument acts on the price or on income.

\subsection{The welfare measure}
\label{sec:welfare}

We rank scenarios by the discounted utility of a
household drawn at random before the shock. There are three permanent
discount-factor types $i\in\{0,1,2\}$ of equal mass; type-$i$ welfare in a scenario
is
%
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{W}_i=\sum_{t\ge0}\beta_i^{\,t}\,\bigl(\mathbb{E}_0[u(c_{it})]-v(n_t)\bigr),
  \label{eq:welfare}
\end{equation}
%
the expectation over the date-$0$ (pre-shock) cross-section of type $i$; the
distribution then evolves endogenously along the transition, so \eqref{eq:welfare}
is the expected discounted utility of a household placed behind a date-$0$ veil of
ignorance, the object a welfarist planner evaluates.
\citet{auclert2023energy}, the model we extend, gauge the distributional effects of energy
policy through consumption inequality; we complement that with a
consumption-equivalent welfare ranking. We report $\mathcal{W}_i$ type by type and,
as a population summary, the aggregate consumption-equivalent variation defined below. Felicity $u$
is \eqref{eq:felicity}, evaluated on the bundle the household consumes, so
$\mathbb{E}_0[u(c_{it})]$ is the exact cross-sectional average, not felicity at the
average bundle.

\emph{Labour is not a household choice.} The union sets the wage \eqref{eq:wpc} and
hours $n_t$ are common across households: a household of productivity $e$ earns
$e\,(1-\tau^L)w\,n_t$ and works the same hours as everyone else. Preferences are
separable and $n_t$ is taken as given, so the labour-disutility term
$v(n)=v_\varphi\,n^{1+\varphi}/(1+\varphi)$ does not enter the household's
consumption--saving problem \eqref{eq:coh}. It is nonetheless a real utility cost of
the hours worked, common to every type, so the welfare criterion must include it: we
subtract $v(n_t)$ in \eqref{eq:welfare}. Omitting it would score a policy that raises
employment as a free lunch: higher consumption with no offsetting disutility of
hours.

\paragraph{From the transition to the CEV.} Along a scenario's transition the
felicity deviations are $\widehat{u}_{it}=u_{it}-u^{\mathrm{ss}}_i$, where
$u_{it}=\mathbb{E}_t[u(c_{it})]$ is average type-$i$ felicity at date $t$ and
$u^{\mathrm{ss}}_i$ its reference-steady-state level; the labour term deviates by
$\Delta v_t=v'(n^{\mathrm{ss}})\,\widehat{n}_t$, with
$v'(n^{\mathrm{ss}})=v_\varphi\,(n^{\mathrm{ss}})^{\varphi}$. The scenario's welfare
relative to its own steady state is
%
\begin{equation}
  \Delta\mathcal{W}_i=\sum_{t\ge0}\beta_i^{\,t}\,\bigl(\widehat{u}_{it}-\Delta v_t\bigr).
  \label{eq:dW}
\end{equation}
%
We express this in consumption units through the consumption-equivalent variation
$\chi_i$ \citep{Lucas1987}: the permanent proportional consumption supplement that,
granted to every type-$i$ household in the reference steady state, reproduces
$\Delta\mathcal{W}_i$. A permanent supplement scales felicity by
$(1+\chi_i)^{1-\sigma}$ and so raises welfare by
$[(1+\chi_i)^{1-\sigma}-1]\,u^{\mathrm{ss}}_i/(1-\beta_i)$; equating to
$\Delta\mathcal{W}_i$ and inverting,
%
\begin{equation}
  \chi_i=
  \begin{cases}
    \exp\!\bigl[(1-\beta_i)\,\Delta\mathcal{W}_i\bigr]-1, & \sigma=1,\\[4pt]
    \Bigl[\,1+(1-\beta_i)\,\Delta\mathcal{W}_i/u^{\mathrm{ss}}_i\,\Bigr]^{1/(1-\sigma)}-1, & \sigma\neq1.
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:cev}
\end{equation}
%
We report the per-type $\chi_i$ as the distributional object and, as the population
figure, the aggregate consumption-equivalent variation $\chi^{\ast}$: the common
permanent supplement that, granted to every type in the reference steady state,
reproduces the equal-weighted sum $\sum_i\Delta\mathcal{W}_i$,
%
\begin{equation}
  \chi^{\ast}=
  \begin{cases}
    \exp\!\bigl[\,\textstyle\sum_i\Delta\mathcal{W}_i\big/\sum_i(1-\beta_i)^{-1}\,\bigr]-1, & \sigma=1,\\[4pt]
    \bigl[\,1+\textstyle\sum_i\Delta\mathcal{W}_i\big/\sum_i u^{\mathrm{ss}}_i(1-\beta_i)^{-1}\,\bigr]^{1/(1-\sigma)}-1, & \sigma\neq1.
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:cev_star}
\end{equation}
%
The figure $\chi^{\ast}$ aggregates in common consumption units. A mean of the
per-type $\chi_i$ would not: each $\chi_i$ is a share of a \emph{different} type's
consumption, and averaging such shares is not a social-welfare aggregate
\citep{DavilaSchaab2025}. Both are in percent of permanent consumption; a negative
value is a loss, the share of consumption a household would forgo in perpetuity to
avoid the scenario.\footnote{The deviations in \eqref{eq:dW} are first order in the shock, so $\chi_i$ is the leading term of the welfare expansion around the (distorted) steady state along a deterministic transition. The setting is not the stochastic one in which a first-order evaluation can reverse welfare rankings \citep{KimKim2003,BenignoWoodford2005}: the transition is deterministic and the first-order term is nonzero and dominant. Because the shock is large and the adoption margin is convex, we bound the neglected second-order terms by recomputing every $\chi_i$ on the nonlinear transition (Appendix~\ref{sec:nlcev}); the levels move by at most about $5\%$ and no ranking changes.}

\paragraph{A common reference across steady states.} Scenarios that begin from
different steady states cannot be compared through \eqref{eq:dW} alone: the
\emph{ex-post} crisis instruments start at the pre-crisis baseline, whereas the
\emph{ex-ante} carbon tax has already moved the economy to a greener, permanently
distorted steady state before the crisis. We fix the baseline steady state as the
common reference and split each scenario's welfare into a standing and a transition
term,
%
\begin{equation}
  \Delta\mathcal{W}_i=
  \underbrace{\frac{u^{\mathrm{pre}}_i-u^{\mathrm{base}}_i-\Delta v^{\mathrm{lvl}}}{1-\beta_i}}_{\text{standing gap vs.\ baseline}}
  +\underbrace{\sum_{t\ge0}\beta_i^{\,t}\bigl(\widehat{u}_{it}-\Delta v_t\bigr)}_{\text{crisis transition}},
  \label{eq:cev_total}
\end{equation}
%
where $u^{\mathrm{pre}}_i$ is felicity at the scenario's own pre-crisis steady state,
$u^{\mathrm{base}}_i$ at the baseline, and
$\Delta v^{\mathrm{lvl}}=v'(n^{\mathrm{base}})\,(n^{\mathrm{pre}}-n^{\mathrm{base}})$
the standing labour-disutility difference. The standing gap is zero for scenarios
that start at the baseline and negative for the ex-ante tax, which gives up
steady-state consumption to raise adoption; carrying it into $\Delta\mathcal{W}_i$
places ex-ante and ex-post policies on a single welfare axis. We feed
\eqref{eq:cev_total} through \eqref{eq:cev} exactly as for any other scenario.
